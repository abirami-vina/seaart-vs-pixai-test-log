Anyone weighing SeaArt vs PixAI will quickly notice that both AI image generators can turn a prompt into polished anime art in seconds. On one hand, SeaArt is a broad creative platform with a large model library and separate AI apps for tasks like character references and editing.

On the other hand, [PixAI](https://eap.pixai.art/go/abirami) is built around anime and character work, and its latest model, [Tsubaki.3](https://eap.pixai.art/go/abirami2), handles generation, references, and edits in one workflow. On paper, both look capable. The real differences only show up when you put them to work on a real anime project.

So we did exactly that, with one original character (OC) and four tests. Her name is Grandma Ume, a retired swordswoman with a white bun, a wooden hairpin, and a katana she leans on like a cane. Each platform created her from a written design, placed her in a complex scene, carried her into new situations, and edited one image twice in a row.

## SeaArt vs PixAI: What Are We Comparing?

To keep this anime AI generator comparison fair, we picked one setup on each platform and ran the same four tests on both. Both platforms have lots of models to choose from, so a different setup might give you different results.

Here's a closer look at what we used and how we tested:

- **PixAI Setup:** Tsubaki.3, PixAI's latest image model, handled every test, including creating Ume, placing her in new scenes from a reference image, and editing her. For one extra step, we also used [PixAI Edit Pro](https://blog.pixai.art/en/pixai-edit-pro-ai-image-editor/), PixAI's editing model, inside [PixAI Studio](https://blog.pixai.art/en/how-to-use-pixai-studio-from-character-photoshoots-to-ai-animation-10-templates-you-can-clone-instantly/), a workspace that links images and edits on one canvas.
- **SeaArt Setup:** Nova Anime AM, an anime model on SeaArt, handled the first two tests. For the reference and editing tests, we used two of SeaArt's AI apps, Character Reference and an image editing app. These are standalone tools that don't show which model runs behind them.
- **Account Tiers:** We used a paid PixAI plan, though most steps also work on the free tier. SeaArt ran on its free tier.
- **Shared Tasks:** Each platform created Ume from the same design, placed her in one complex scene, brought her into two new situations, and edited one image twice.
- **Test Date and Attempts:** Every image was generated on September 28, 2026, and we kept the first result for each test. The one exception was SeaArt's first character image, which needed a switch to an anime model and two prompt revisions, as covered in the next section.

The early verdict is that Tsubaki.3 kept Ume closest to her original design from start to finish, while SeaArt produced bolder images and followed some scene instructions more literally. Neither platform won every test, so the better choice depends on whether consistency or visual impact comes first for you.

## Anime Image Quality in PixAI vs SeaArt: Creating One OC

Before generating anything, we wrote out exactly what Ume should look like, so we'd have a checklist to judge every image against.

She's in her seventies, with white hair in a low bun, a wooden hairpin, warm brown eyes, laugh lines, and a faint scar on her chin. Her outfit is a faded indigo kimono with a gray obi and straw sandals. She also keeps round reading glasses on a cord around her neck and leans on a sheathed katana like a walking stick.

That added up to 11 traits we checked in every image. Since Tsubaki.3 understands natural language, we described Ume the way you'd brief an illustrator.

```text
An elderly Japanese woman in her seventies, a retired swordswoman, stands upright and faces the camera with a calm, knowing smile. Her white hair is pulled into a neat low bun at the nape of her neck and held in place by a single wooden hairpin. She has warm brown eyes, deep laugh lines at the corners of her eyes, and a faint pale scar across her chin. She wears a faded indigo kimono tied with a plain gray obi and straw sandals. A pair of round reading glasses hangs from a thin cord around her neck and rests against her chest. Her right hand rests on the hilt of a sheathed katana planted tip-down beside her like a walking stick. Full body shot at eye level, centered, plain off-white background. Soft daylight from the upper left, gentle shadows falling to her right. Anime illustration, crisp clean linework, soft two-tone cel shading, muted natural palette.
```

Meanwhile, Nova Anime AM responds best to Danbooru-style tags, so we translated the same design into a tag list for SeaArt.

```text
masterpiece, best quality, amazing quality, very aesthetic, newest, anime, anime coloring, cel shading, lineart, 2d, illustration, 1girl, solo, old woman, old, wrinkled skin, white hair, single hair bun, wooden hair stick, brown eyes, scar on chin, round eyewear, glasses chain, glasses around neck, japanese clothes, kimono, dark blue kimono, faded clothes, grey sash, obi, straw sandals, zouri, katana, sheathed, hand on hilt, leaning on sword, sword planted in ground, standing, full body, looking at viewer, closed mouth, smile, simple background, white background, soft lighting
```

These were the outputs we got.

Tsubaki.3 held 10 of the 11 traits on its first attempt, including the glasses resting against her chest and the katana planted tip-down under her hand. It missed the chin scar and lit her from the right instead of the upper left. The muted palette and soft cel shading make it a clean reference image with no extra details. Her calm smile, relaxed hands, and upright posture also came through naturally, with no anatomy issues.

SeaArt took more adjustments. Its first attempt came out photographic until we switched to an anime checkpoint, and the tag list needed two revisions before the design matched. The final image has sharper linework and richer color.

Her face and hands look natural, and the smile reads as warm and confident. It fully held eight traits, but it read "faded clothes" as a dip-dyed white hem, added a second pair of glasses on her face, and introduced red sandal straps and a red sword cord that no prompt requested.

Both images look great. But if you plan to reuse a character, sticking to the design counts for more than looks, because any extra detail the model adds will follow her into every future image. We put that to the test later in this article, when we brought Ume into new scenes using these images as references.

We were also surprised by how both platforms handled her age. Anime models often make older characters look younger, so we expected Ume to lose a few years, but both platforms kept her clearly elderly.

## How Well Does Tsubaki.3 Follow Detailed Anime Prompts?

A single character on a plain background is fairly easy to generate. A scene with two people, a camera angle, a light source, and small props is where instructions start competing.

We asked both platforms to show Ume blocking a teenage student's overhead strike in a dojo at sunset, shot from floor level, with her glasses swinging on their cord and a steaming teacup in the lower-left foreground.

Following the Tsubaki.3 [prompt guide](https://blog.pixai.art/en/tsubaki-3-prompt-guide/), the prompt spells out how each element relates to the others, from who strikes whom to where the light comes from. SeaArt received the same scene as a tag list, using "1girl, 1boy" in place of solo tags.

```text
Inside an old wooden dojo at dusk, an elderly Japanese woman in her seventies blocks a teenage boy's overhead strike with the sheathed katana she holds horizontally above her head in both hands. She has white hair in a neat low bun held by a wooden hairpin, warm brown eyes, laugh lines, and a faint scar on her chin. She wears a faded indigo kimono with a plain gray obi and straw sandals. Round reading glasses swing on a cord around her neck from the force of the block. The boy wears a white keikogi and dark blue hakama and grips a wooden bokken with both hands, his face tense and surprised while she smiles calmly. A steaming teacup sits on the tatami in the foreground at the bottom left of the frame. Low-angle shot from floor level, the two figures in the middle ground with a row of paper shoji screens behind them. Warm orange sunset light pours through the shoji from behind the boy and casts long shadows of both figures toward the camera, while the rest of the dojo falls into cool blue shade. Anime illustration, crisp clean linework, soft two-tone cel shading, muted natural palette.
```

And here's the result we got back.

<img width="2250" height="1030" alt="Two anime dojo scenes of an elderly swordswoman and a young student at sunset." src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/e93cb9bc-4f53-4a43-9029-9952eed5b75a" />

*The same dojo scene produced a staged overhead block with Tsubaki.3 on PixAI (left) and a dynamic sword-drawing pose with Nova Anime AM on SeaArt (right).*

Tsubaki.3 followed most of the scene. It staged a clear overhead block, placed the sunset glow directly behind the shoji with cool shade inside the dojo, and put the steaming teacup exactly in the lower-left corner. Its main slip was merging the katana and bokken into a single wooden weapon that both characters grip, and her glasses hang still instead of swinging.

Whereas SeaArt matched the low camera angle but changed the action entirely, showing Ume in a sword-drawing stance while the student stands back with a sheathed katana. The teacup is missing, the "steam" tag became cartoon puffs above the student's head, and Ume's glasses ended up on the boy.

SeaArt's version has more energy, and some creators will prefer it for a single dramatic illustration. For a comic panel where the action needs to read correctly, Tsubaki.3 followed more of the scene, and its main miss is easy to fix with a targeted edit.

## Character Consistency on PixAI and SeaArt: Keeping Ume On-Model

Describing a character in words only gets you so far when you want them to look the same in every image. So for this [character consistency](https://blog.pixai.art/en/pixai-character-consistency-3-beginner-methods/) test, we gave each platform a reference image, using the first picture of Ume each platform had created.

Adding the reference image works a little differently on each platform. On PixAI, you can drop the image into the prompt box and mention it in your prompt as @image1, all on the same screen where you generate. On SeaArt, the reference goes into a separate Character Reference panel with its own upload and prompt fields.

<img width="2224" height="1150" alt="Interface screenshots of the reference image upload on PixAI and SeaArt." src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/15512dfd-bdab-499e-b628-300f13518eca" />

*PixAI adds a character reference in the prompt box of the generation screen (left), while SeaArt opens a separate Character Reference panel with its own image and prompt fields (right).*

We tested two new scenes, each with its own prompt, and used the exact same wording on both platforms. The first prompt took Ume to an autumn market.

```text
The character from @image1 stands at a fruit stall in a busy autumn street market at midday, laughing as she picks a ripe persimmon from a woven basket. She has moved her round reading glasses from the cord onto her nose and holds a small handwritten price tag up close in her other hand to read it. Her sheathed katana leans against the wooden stall beside her. Framed from mid-thigh up at eye level. Hard midday sunlight from directly overhead casts short shadows under the stall awning, with orange and red maple trees behind her. Keep her wooden hairpin, faded indigo kimono, and gray obi exactly as in the reference, in the same anime art style.
```

The second prompt placed her on a quiet veranda at night.

```text
The character from @image1 sits on the edge of the wooden veranda of an old Japanese house at night, her feet resting on a flat stone step, eyes half closed with a peaceful smile, holding a small teacup in both hands. Her sheathed katana rests horizontally across her lap, and her round reading glasses hang from their cord against her chest. A full moon in the upper right casts cool blue-white light across her shoulders and the veranda boards, while a paper lantern on the floor beside her throws a small warm orange glow onto her face and hands. Full-body side view from a slightly high angle, with a dark garden and a stone lantern behind her. Keep her wooden hairpin, faded indigo kimono, gray obi, and straw sandals exactly as in the reference, in the same anime art style.
```

And here are the images we generated.

<img width="2090" height="1860" alt="A two-by-two grid of an elderly anime swordswoman at a fruit market and on a veranda at night." src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/734f2dbc-8c0d-4b44-b394-1c86b1f81751" />

*Grandma Ume carried into an autumn market and a moonlit veranda from each platform's own reference image, with Tsubaki.3 on PixAI (left) and SeaArt's Character Reference app (right).*

On both platforms, Ume looks like the same person in all four images. The differences show up in the smaller details.

Across both scenes, Tsubaki.3 kept Ume's face, muted indigo kimono, wooden hairpin, and soft rendering style closest to the original. In the market scene, it moved her glasses from the cord to her nose exactly as asked. In the veranda scene, it placed the paper lantern on the ground below the veranda instead of beside her.

SeaArt's reference app followed the scene details more literally. In the market scene, it delivered bright overhead sun, though it framed her full-body instead of from mid-thigh. In the veranda scene, it put the katana across her lap and the lantern glowing right beside her, as requested.

The trade-off is style. In both scenes, the backgrounds shifted toward a painterly, near-photographic look, and the market image kept its reference's dip-dyed hem and red sword cord.

The veranda scene also produced the most unexpected result. SeaArt swapped the red-strapped geta from its reference image for the straw sandals named in the prompt, letting the text override the image.

## Editing an Anime Image Twice: Tsubaki.3 vs SeaArt

Regenerating an image from scratch risks losing what you liked, so the final test took each platform's veranda image through two edits in a row. The first edit opened Ume's eyes and changed her expression.

The second edit, made on that result, turned the night into a snowy winter morning. On PixAI, Tsubaki.3 handled both edits on the same screen, while on SeaArt each edit opened a separate image editing panel.

Here's a closer look at the prompts we used to make these edits.

```text
Open her eyes and change her expression to a warm, gentle smile as she looks up toward the moon, with warm brown eyes. Keep her face shape, white hair bun, wooden hairpin, round reading glasses on their cord, faded indigo kimono, gray obi, teacup, sheathed katana, straw sandals, pose, lighting, background, and art style unchanged.
```

```text
Change the night scene to an early winter morning with light snow falling. A thin layer of snow covers the garden, the stone lantern, and the edge of the veranda; the moon is gone, and the sky is pale gray. Soft overcast daylight from the left replaces the moonlight, and the paper lantern is unlit. Keep the character exactly as she is, including her open eyes and smile, glasses on their cord, kimono, obi, teacup, sheathed katana, and sandals, and keep the composition and art style unchanged.
```

This is what both platforms returned.

*The same veranda image taken through two edits in a row, opening her eyes and then turning the night into a snowy morning, with Tsubaki.3 on PixAI (top) and SeaArt's image editing app (bottom).*

Here's a closer look at what each edit changed and what it kept:

- **Requested Changes:** Both platforms opened her eyes in a brown shade and delivered the snow, pale sky, and missing moon. SeaArt went further with the snow, covering the veranda edge, while Tsubaki.3 left the boards dry.
- **Stable Details:** Tsubaki.3 kept the katana, teacup, and composition through both edits and switched the lantern off as asked. SeaArt held its composition almost pixel for pixel but left the lantern glowing under a cap of snow.
- **Unrequested Changes:** Tsubaki.3 added a second pair of glasses on her face in the first edit. SeaArt removed the katana entirely and turned the gentle smile into a broad laugh on a younger, rounder face.
- **Drift Across Steps:** Neither platform introduced new problems in the second edit. Whatever changed in the first edit carried forward exactly, so the first pass is the one to check closely.

That last point also shows when regenerating becomes the better option. A stray object, like the extra glasses, is a quick targeted fix. A changed identity trait, like SeaArt's softened face, is harder to edit back, and starting again from the reference is usually more practical.

## SeaArt vs PixAI Pricing: Free Access, Credits, and Practical Limits

Both platforms let you start for free, but they charge in different currencies, so the numbers below aren't directly comparable.

PixAI runs on credits, and free accounts get 10,000 credits a day, with optional paid plans for more. SeaArt gives free users 130 stamina a day. Stamina resets daily and doesn't carry over, and once it runs out, SeaArt draws from credits, which you buy or earn separately. Our [SeaArt alternative](https://medium.com/@abirami.vina/exploring-a-seaart-ai-alternative-built-for-anime-inside-pixai-fe391e15df3f) guide covers how stamina and credits work in more detail.

In our PixAI vs SeaArt tests, Tsubaki.3 used 4,000 credits per image at 1152×864, rising to 4,900 for an image with a reference or an edit. On SeaArt, a Nova Anime AM image cost 8 stamina, a Character Reference image cost 45, and each edit cost 15. Everything we ran on SeaArt worked on the free tier, and every Tsubaki.3 step we used is also available to free PixAI accounts.

Two practical limits stood out more than price. SeaArt's reference and editing apps don't name their underlying model, so the look can shift between steps, as it did in the continuation test. Those tools also open in separate panels, which adds a context switch to every step of a multi-image project. On PixAI, the reference, prompt, edits, and Studio stayed in one place throughout.

## SeaArt vs PixAI: Which Anime AI Generator Should You Choose?

Across four tests, each platform showed its own strengths, and seeing them side by side tells you more than any single image. Tsubaki.3 kept Ume's face, outfit, and art style intact from her first image to her last, which is what a recurring character needs most. SeaArt followed some scene instructions boldly, but her design shifted more along the way.

Here's a closer look at the situations where PixAI with Tsubaki.3 is the better fit:

- **Recurring Characters:** If you're creating an OC you'll draw again and again, like a comic lead or a VTuber persona, Tsubaki.3 is a strong fit. It kept Ume's face, outfit, and art style closest to her original design in every test, even after two rounds of edits.
- **Scene Accuracy:** When a scene has several moving parts, Tsubaki.3 follows more of your instructions. In our dojo test, it placed the teacup, camera angle, and sunset light exactly where the prompt asked.
- **Connected Workflow:** Uploading references, making edits, and turning an image into a manga page all happen in one place on PixAI, so you spend less time jumping between tools.
- **Faithful Starting Images:** Tsubaki.3's first image of Ume stuck closely to the written design, with no extra details added. That means fewer surprises to carry forward each time you reuse the image as a reference.

By contrast, SeaArt may suit you if you want bold, high-contrast single illustrations or enjoy working directly with tag-based checkpoints and a wide mix of models. Its reference app followed scene placement closely in our continuation test, and its editor held composition tightly.

However, if you've been looking for a SeaArt alternative because your characters drift between images, Tsubaki.3 was the steadier option in our testing.

Ultimately, the direct answer to SeaArt vs PixAI depends on the job. For creating, reusing, and editing the same anime character, PixAI with Tsubaki.3 is the better fit based on what we saw. For eye-catching one-off images with extra stylistic punch, SeaArt holds its own.
